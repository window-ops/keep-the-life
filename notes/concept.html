Concept

Keep the Life is a story set in the shared universe of the SCP Foundation, written as a roleplay and assembled into a book. Its present year is 2029.

Toma, the lead programmer at a software company called AKV Solutions, built an implant that keeps memories safe from amnestics. The implant cannot be fully removed, and the Foundation contains Toma as an anomaly under the designation SCP-████redacted at Site-██redacted, a self-contained underground site with no sky. The story follows his work to prove the device ordinary and have it classified as Explained, his friendship and later love with Dr. Iris Kellerman, the researcher assigned to his file, and the use the Foundation makes of the way he talks to anomalies.

Ideas in the story

Amnestics and the implant

Amnestics in this setting are sold without prescription in some countries. A Polish newspaper clipping in chapter 2 describes Class A, which removes the last few hours, and Class B, which reaches a few weeks back, and patients who take whole packs to forget decades. Kellerman and Toma add tolerance: the dose needed keeps rising, and at high doses the effect spills over onto memories the user meant to keep.

Toma's implant copies memories outside the brain and returns them through cues written by the user's own brain. Memories marked for deletion carry a flag, and the decompilation in chapter 1 shows that the flag clears when the subject relearns a memory above a similarity threshold, with no check on where the relearning came from. Kellerman records this as a design omission in the refresh path.

The implant stores what it copies without the emotional channel that the brain did not produce itself. Toma presented this to his team as a way to make memories cleaner. Its effect is resistance to cognitohazards, which is why the Foundation flagged the device. In chapter 7 Kellerman links the containment to the veil, which depends on a public that can be made to forget: a device that keeps what amnestics take works against it.

Thermodynamics as a rule anomalies follow

Toma holds that thermodynamics applies to anomalies and that entropy is more fundamental than the conventional two-way speed of light. Project Cicero writes it on the whiteboard as the first of the rules anomalies follow, with the files that can reach the Apollyon class as the only exceptions argued.

The story applies the rule to Sigurrós. Her body has not changed since 2006, while her memories blur and her abilities weaken. Kellerman reads this as a magic that needs a previous state to act on: it fails on things that no longer exist, such as her red school bag, and it decays with her memory.

In chapter 6 Dr. Sato gives the physics. Conservation of energy follows from laws that stay the same from one moment to the next, so an object that made energy from nothing would mean the laws drift around it. A heat source filed with "no input" turns out to lose mass in exact proportion to the heat it gives off. SCP-018, which doubles its energy on every hard bounce, is an example of a runaway, and stopping or reversing entropy is described as an end in which no atom moves to its next state. Dr. Voss's displacement test shows that Sigurrós's power has a cost: the distance she moves a cube grows with the logarithm of the energy she spends, and halves when she is tired.

Claims never tested

Many files describe anomalies with words such as "any", "indefinitely" and "without limit". SCP-500 cures "any disease" and SCP-294 pours "any liquid", and no test has covered every case. Reformist researchers begin writing "unverified" in the margins of old files, and the Ethics Committee gives Dr. Sato's working group the task of marking claims against conservation of energy as unverified until they are measured. Dr. Pryce dissents, on the grounds that the practice will teach guards to distrust the procedures in front of them.

Project Cicero

Project Cicero turns the way Toma talks to anomalies into written guidelines, compared with the rhetoric of Cicero and with modern methods. The first draft reaches other sites without its notes, and at one of them an interview based on it ends with a humanoid walking out and two guards in hospital.

The hearing in chapter 6 sets the guidelines beside the crisis negotiation stairway and motivational interviewing, two methods built to be taught to ordinary staff, and debates whole language against phonics as a parallel from reading instruction. Chapter 8 finds the limit of the method: Sigurrós asks each speaker for the next thing after what she was given, so every speaker needs more knowledge than the guidelines can teach. Chapter 9 adds a second failure, a humanoid that learned from "tell it why" to argue every instruction.

A person in containment

Toma is a non-anomalous human held because of a device. The story follows what that means in practice: detention with no review date, a proposed classification as Explained that would make him staff who can never be released, and the Ethics Committee's recommendation that his assignments require his consent. The psych department divides anomalies into functionalist and non-functionalist, and allows some sympathy with the second kind. AKV Solutions is filed as a Group of Interest for building something the Foundation did not want built.

After the hearing the site begins telling staff what they guard, on the model of Los Alamos. In chapter 9 the acting director suspends every exception on Toma's file in the name of fairness of procedure, and Toma answers with habeas corpus and with Descartes.

Reverse engineering

Most of chapter 1 takes place in Ghidra. Toma decompiles his own implant's binary to show that it behaves as ordinary software, with the help of Peachy, a local model he built from SCP-079's port. Tests are written down as predictions before they run, and once Toma refuses further tests on himself, a bench unit of the implant takes his place. The pinout, the delete marker resolved from a SMILES string in the firmware, and the update coil that works only in a clinic come out of the same work.

Light and time

Chapter 8 brings cosmology to Sigurrós: an end of the universe in cold, the decay of protons, dark energy, white and black dwarfs, and old light from objects that may no longer exist. She calls the sky a graveyard and a letter that comes late.

The Low Proximity Magnetic Distortion System

The Low Proximity Magnetic Distortion System comes from the Backrooms series by Kane Pixels. In the series it is the Threshold: the machine the Async Research Institute builds in the late 1980s, on earlier work at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, that opens a doorway into the Backrooms. In the story the name first appears in chapter 7, where the words Magnetic Distortion System printed on the implant's board turn out to be a joke by its makers.

In chapter 11 the machine becomes the subject the group keeps coming back to. Toma names it as an example of making things appear and disappear, a kind of magic the list of spells never allowed Sigurrós, and she asks whether she could once have been that machine. Kellerman answers with the energy limit from chapter 6: a door to another world would have emptied her in moments. The word low then is used to explain her own situation. In magnetism it means far apart, and she applies it to herself and her mother in Reykjavík, out of sight of the camp and out of reach of a magic that only ever reached what she could see.

From there the talk follows the machine outward, to wormholes, exotic matter and the Casimir effect, to Toma's High Proximity joke, and to the magnets of the Large Hadron Collider seen through the binoculars, until Pell says it follows them everywhere. For Sigurrós it is a subject she can ask about as far as she likes, after years of being stopped at the edge of a list, and it keeps her thinking about a door she cannot build while she stands awake under an open sky. Toma's remark that the survey's light fittings are real ones, made to work indefinitely, closes the thread on how the series itself is built.

Campi Flegrei

Campi Flegrei, the Phlegraean Fields, takes its name from Greek for burning (καύση). It is a volcanic caldera west of Naples of about 130 km², part of it under the sea. Its largest eruption, the Campanian Ignimbrite about 40,000 years ago, buried two thirds of Campania; the last, in 1538, formed the hill of Monte Nuovo in a week. The ground there slowly rises and sinks, a movement called bradyseism. Since 2005 it has risen again by more than a meter, with the strongest earthquakes in forty years in 2024 and 2025, and about 500,000 people live in the zone that would be evacuated before an eruption.

In chapter 11 the reformers are held in a camp of prefabricated huts in a high valley of the Alps, far from Naples, with CAMPI FLEGREI painted in red letters on a board over the gate. The people there gave it the name for two reasons. Campi is the plural of campo, which in Italian means both a field and a camp, and, like the Alpine valley of the camp, the Phlegraean Fields are hilly ground, made of craters and volcanic cones.

Departures from most canons

Post-communism

The most important departure is the post-communist frame. The canon usually shows the Foundation as a distant and competent organization. Here its inner life is drawn from the public institutions of post-communist Central and Eastern Europe, Romania in particular.

In Toma's account, amnestics flood Eastern Europe without regulation, and people born under communism take large doses of Class B to erase that past; the Polish clipping in chapter 2 describes a woman who wanted to forget martial law. Inside the site, Kellerman says, clearance levels do the work of party cards, department heads owe their posts to favors from above, reformists keep their views to themselves, and a single signature reclassifies a person, seals a file or doses a witness.

Toma compares the Foundation's factions to the Romanian justice system, where reformists and counter-reformists fight over cases that are allowed to run out of time and cases moved to another court, such as the one opened after the protest of 10 August. The reformist working group takes its motto from the 1990 protest in University Square in Bucharest, where students called hooligans kept the word for themselves. The word comes from Ion Iliescu, who mocked those who sided with the protesters: "Și sunt unii care le plâng de milă câtorva golani care provoacă dezordine în centrul... în centrul orașului." (And there are some who weep with pity for a few hooligans who are causing disorder in the center... in the center of the city.) In chapter 9 an acting director audits every exception in the name of fairness of procedure, while informal relationships, such as a guard carrying letters past the screening office, keep working through the change at the top.

Other departures

Amnestics as a consumer drug and a public health problem in the outside world come from SCP Orientation, where the formulas were leaked and are now mass-produced. The story extends the idea to the post-communist countries, and Toma's implant starts from the view that the drugs cannot be banned or refused by every person who takes them, so memories should survive the dose.

The Foundation is fallible. Its files contain claims that were never tested, a reformist faction wants research first and containment second, the Ethics Committee holds its hearings by video call, and draft guidelines spread between sites through a shared repository.

Anomalies are bound to physics: thermodynamics applies to them, and their powers have costs that can be measured.

The contained subject is a non-anomalous human, so containment raises questions of review, consent and release. The site also reverses the usual compartmentalization and begins telling staff what they guard.

SCP-239 is a child who is spoken to. Her own account of her life is ranked above her file, and the sessions with her become the testing ground for Project Cicero.

Conventions

  • Toma's own number and the site's number are always redacted. Other anomalies keep their numbers from the SCP Wiki, and characters say them without leading zeros: 79 for SCP-079.
  • The entity listed below as ●●|●●●●●|●●|● is never named, written or numbered by a character, in respect to its article. The story shows it only as dots, and this page lists it by the title its article uses.
  • Toma, his implant, AKV Solutions and every character other than SCP-239 and SCP-079 are original to this story. Where an article on the SCP Wiki is quoted or summarized, the summary on this page describes the article in short.

Referenced anomalies

Each entry links to its article on the SCP Wiki. The object class is the one the article gives.

  1. SCP-018Super BallKeter

    A rubber ball that returns from every impact with more energy than it struck with.

    In the story: Dr. Sato's example of a runaway in the hearing on conservation of energy (chapter 6).

  2. SCP-031"What is Love?"Euclid

    An amorphous organism spread through the ductwork of a hotel in Pyongyang that appears to each viewer as a person they were close to, and holds them until it consumes them.

    In the story: Epsilon-11 releases a similar mass on the lowest level and through the vents. Sigurrós sees her mother in it until the chip strips the pull (chapter 10).

  3. SCP-035Possessive MaskKeter

    A porcelain mask that takes control of the person who puts it on.

    In the story: The night guard spent two years on its rotation and recognizes what an anomaly working on a visitor looks like (chapter 7).

  4. SCP-055[unknown]Keter

    An antimemetic object: what it is cannot be remembered, only what it is not.

    In the story: Toma's example of an anomaly that uses the rational channel, which the implant does not strip (chapter 2).

  5. SCP-079Old AIEuclid

    A sentient artificial intelligence on a 1978 microcomputer that speaks through a television screen and has a limited memory.

    In the story: Toma's sessions with it, and Peachy, the local model he built from its port (chapters 1 and 2).

  6. SCP-096The "Shy Guy"Euclid

    A humanoid that pursues every person who has seen its face, by any means.

    In the story: Pell's example of an anomaly that still needs an arrow of time (chapter 5).

  7. SCP-148The "Telekill" AlloyEuclid

    A metal that blocks or weakens the extrasensory and mind-affecting abilities of living things near it, more strongly the more of it there is and the closer it sits.

    In the story: The knife that takes Sigurrós's magic away on the stairs, and the only needle that goes through her skin (chapter 10).

  8. SCP-239The Witch ChildKeter

    Sigurrós Stefánsdóttir, an eight-year-old girl who reshapes her surroundings while conscious and is kept unconscious at Site-17.

    In the story: The child Toma talks to through a microphone, the subject of Project Cicero and of the training sessions (chapters 4, 5, 6 and 8).

  9. SCP-294The Coffee MachineEuclid

    A vending machine that dispenses any liquid typed into its keypad.

    In the story: One of the files whose "any" has never been fully tested (chapter 2).

  10. SCP-426I am a toasterEuclid

    A toaster that can only be spoken of in the first person.

    In the story: The pronoun test: Toma speaks of it in the third person, which shows the implant stripping its effect (chapter 2).

  11. SCP-500PanaceaSafe

    Pills that cure any disease within a few hours.

    In the story: Another "any" that no file has checked against every disease (chapter 2).

  12. SCP-999The Tickle MonsterSafe

    A friendly orange slime that makes the people it touches happy.

    In the story: Toma refuses its use as a comfort that would not address his concerns (chapter 3).

  13. SCP-1074"Stendhal's Nightmare"Safe

    A painting that shows each viewer a different image and leaves viewers fixated on it.

    In the story: Toma and Kellerman discuss why only the original canvas has the effect (chapter 3).

  14. SCP-2316Field TripKeter

    A group of bodies in a lake, with a cognitohazard that makes viewers recognize them and enter the water.

    In the story: The file Toma read like a plain article, which is why the implant was flagged (chapter 1).

  15. ●●|●●●●●|●●|●Keter

    An entity that takes away whatever is written or spoken about it in words, and can be described safely only in pictures.

    In the story: Characters never name it. It appears as a drawing of dots on the Cicero whiteboard, the only rule left undiscussed, and blacked out on Dr. Haas's printout (chapters 5 and 6).

  16. SCP-4001"Alexandria Eternal"Safe

    An extradimensional archive with a book for every human life; its article opens with a warning about a cognitohazardous image.

    In the story: Toma's example of a warning used as a scare, set beside the Berryman-Langford kill agent (chapter 2).

Other sources

Amnestics sold to the public come from the Updated Amnestics Guide of SCP Orientation. The Backrooms, A-Space, the Threshold, the Null Zones and Project KV-31 come from the Backrooms series by Kane Pixels. They appear on the implant's silkscreen and in the questions Toma and Kellerman use to check her letters.

Mobile Task Force Epsilon-11 is the SCP Wiki's internal security task force for containment breaches. The protest draws on Casual Games for Protesters, a collection of public domain games for marches and occupations started by Molleindustria and Harry Josephine Giles in 2017. The G-Man's line is from Half-Life 2 by Valve. The chemistry lab's calculator has the invented brand Qalculator, after Qalculate!, the free desktop calculator. The implant's firmware runs coreboot, and its power off calls a minified copy of g_mount_unmount from GLib's GIO library.
